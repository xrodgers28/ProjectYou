**What this is:** the reasoning under ProjectYou: why the life sections are the sections they are, which thinkers and frameworks the system is built on, and where the habit, measurement and reflection modules came from.

**Read this when:** you are about to add, rename, merge or delete a life section, a tracker category, a card format or a habit rule, and you need to know what the existing shape was arguing for before you change it.

**Last verified:** 2026-08-16, by reading the eight source documents this file replaces plus four project memory files. Almost everything here is source material rather than live state, so the facts are stable: a book does not change what it argued, and a decision made on Aug 9, 2026 stays made. The one exception is the visual identity of the life sections. Re-read that file rather than trusting the table if the colors matter to what you are doing.

## 1. The premise

Project YOU is one person's answer to a specific problem, and it is worth keeping that shape in mind, because the system reads as a generic wellness product only if you remove the reason it was made.

The one-line definition, used throughout the source deck:

> "A gamified operating manual that turns your life goals into an actionable program."

The longer version, also from the deck:

> "A simple system that helps you take control of your actions, to get to the outcomes you desire."

And the plain-language framing: a personal system for living a happier, longer life, where you decide which parts of your life matter most, set what you want them to look like, and the system helps you build the daily habits that get you there.

### Where it came from

The setup is mortality math. A life is about 5,000 weeks. Scott's 50th birthday landed flat, and he took stock of things he was not happy with: drinking more than he wanted, too much time in front of a screen, too much doom-scrolling, missing real contact with people, "mortgaging my life for something in the future." The line the whole project hangs on:

> "My life was on auto-pilot but I had never set the direction I was headed."

The change started with one thing, his diet, and then compounded. He began noticing what gave him energy, going to bed on time, working from a new place each week, saying small hellos to strangers, taking walks, and tracking what he did in order to get perspective. Curiosity took over and he started collecting insights, quotes and statistics, which is when he noticed the frequency illusion (Baader-Meinhof), the sense that once you notice something you see it everywhere. He made a "parts list" of what he wanted to change, and eventually realized he needed a framework to know where to focus. That framework became the Clarity Compass, and the whole thing became Project YOU.

The health side is anchored by a backcasting exercise: a written list of specific things he wants to still be able to do at 90 (get up off the floor using one arm, lift a suitcase into an overhead bin, pick up grandkids, balance on one leg, climb four flights in three minutes), then work back from that picture to the habits needed now. That is Peter Attia's marginal decade.

Two lines carry the emotional argument:

> "Everything represents a finite time to savor your life. Attention is your truest sense of wealth."

> "Ultimately, you want to be the pilot of your life, not a passenger."

And one standing instruction, which is the reason this document exists at all:

> "Any work on Project YOU should treat it as a real personal operating system, not a generic wellness template."

### The 5,000 weeks ruling

Oliver Burkeman's book says 4,000 weeks. The deck says 5,000. This was checked and deliberately left alone. The Reference Library's ruling, verbatim:

> "the deck uses '5,000 weeks'; the book's figure is 4,000, but Scott keeps 5,000 as his own deliberate framing (decided Aug 9, 2026), so this is intentional, not an error."

Do not correct it.

### The airplane metaphor is a presentation layer

The deck presents everything as building and flying the airplane that is your life. This is the single most misread part of the system, so it is stated as a rule: **the airplane metaphor is a presentation layer only, not part of the core program.** The real program is the Clarity Compass, its seven sections, the habits, the cue cards and the trackers. The flying language can be swapped out or dropped entirely without touching the substance. The glossary below is useful mainly for reading the original 190-slide deck.

| Flight term | What it actually means |
|---|---|
| Mission | The why |
| Parts List | The what (what matters to you, what you want to improve) |
| Blueprint | How it works |
| Assembly Instructions | How to build it |
| Power Source | Energy and fuel |
| Flight Plan | The execution path |
| Clarity Compass | The dashboard you fly by |
| Map | Tracking progress |
| Flight Hours | Experience |
| Ground Crew / Air Traffic Control | Support (coaches, mentors, friends) |
| Control System | Feedback and corrections |
| Maintenance Plan | Sustain and improve |
| Landing Plan | Evaluate and reflect |
| Pilot's Log Book | The running record |

All airplane-themed product names (Take Flight Deck, Cockpit Cards, POH, Flight Deck Drills) are retired. See section 9.

## 2. The life sections

### The Clarity Compass

The Clarity Compass is the personal dashboard for focus, alignment and clarity: a framework that maps aspirations into measurable actions. Visually it is a seven-sided wheel, one colored wedge per section, with a small airplane above it. On the deck's "Flight Controls" slide the same wheel appears as a dial on a cockpit controller.

Seven is not arbitrary and it is not a rounding of some larger list. It is the set that survived the "parts list" exercise: the areas Scott found he was actually trying to change, grouped until each group had its own distinct set of habits, its own trackers and its own evidence base. A section earns a place when it has all three. That is the test to apply before adding an eighth.

The rule that falls out of all of this: **the seven sections are the life model, and every other list of near-seven things is a view with its own job.** Adding to a view does not add to the model, and adding to the model means adding a section with its own habits, its own trackers and its own evidence, which nothing has yet earned.

## 3. The measurement philosophy

### Measuring it changes it

The trackers are not a record-keeping exercise. They are the intervention. The whole argument compresses into three lines from the Quantified Self summary:

> "A measurement layer for your whole life: everything you track, everywhere, flows into one database, and every dashboard is a view of it. Measuring it changes it. Your data becomes your coach."

The mechanism is the Hawthorne effect: people change their behavior simply because they are being observed. Project YOU turns that inward. From the Knowledge Base: observation changes behavior, so the logging itself is the intervention. From the QS summary, stated as a design principle:

> "Hawthorne effect: the moment you measure something, it starts to change. The tracker is the nudge."

Scott's own framing is worth keeping because it explains why the trackers are built the way they are: tracking reinforces habits and keeps him focused on showing up rather than obsessing over outcomes, and the daily five-to-ten-minute rituals compound and replace doom-scrolling. The satisfaction of filling in the grid is the reinforcement. That is why the annual dot-grid format survived from paper into the software.

The movement this borrows from is Quantified Self (Gary Wolf and Kevin Kelly), whose slogan is "self-knowledge through numbers." One further lesson taken from that community, and stated as a design rule:

> "See the benefit sooner: pair each habit with a near-term signal so the payoff is visible fast, not abstract."

### The n=1 experiment framing

> "n=1 experiments: run small self-experiments and let your own data settle them, rather than relying on generic advice."

This is the same idea as the deck's "Pilots": running small life experiments to gather evidence about what to do next, treating your schedule as an experiment you reflect on, refine and improve. The consequence is that the system is allowed to disagree with the literature about Scott specifically, and the tie-breaker is his own log.

### The one-log principle

The single most load-bearing engineering decision in the whole system, and it came out of a design argument rather than a technical one:

> "Every dashboard you have is the same underlying data, sliced a different way. The January grid is Date across the top and Tracker down the side. The annual dot-grids are one tracker across a year. The Time Bandit Wheel is blocks grouped by area for a week. If each of those keeps its own separate data, they drift apart and nothing reconciles."

The fix is one master log where every daily entry lands as a single row, tagged well enough that any dashboard can be built by filtering and pivoting it. One row per tracker per day. Every view is a pivot of that one log:

- Monthly grid: pivot date across, tracker down.
- Annual dot-grid: filter to one tracker, lay the year out, fill each day's dot.
- Time Bandit Wheel outer ring: sum blocks or minutes by group for the day or week.
- By-category time view: the same model scoped to hours by project.

And the rule in one sentence: **you never enter the same fact twice.**

This is why 03-Data-Model.md describes a `qs_log` and not a table per tracker. If you find yourself adding a second store for a new dashboard, you have hit the exact failure this principle was written to prevent.

### The tag-at-source rule

The corollary, and the thing that makes the log self-populating rather than a second chore:

> "This is the part that makes it self-populating. Every daily Habit Bandit to-do is one tracked behavior. If each to-do carries the right tags, closing it out writes a clean row into the master log with no re-entry."

Each habit to-do carries, beyond its text, a Tracker (which metric it maps to), a Group (its band), a Value and Unit where the habit has a number, and Tags where useful (topic, person, place, project). Then the rule that decides everything:

> "Untagged items stay as ordinary to-dos and do not feed a dashboard. The tag is what turns a task into a data point."

Tagging happens at the source, on the to-do, at the moment the behavior is defined. It is never a later reconciliation pass. This is why the habit-name contract matters so much in the live system: the board, the module page, the wheel and the streak all match on the exact `todos.task` string, and renaming one side breaks the chain silently. The contract table is in 03-Data-Model.md.

### Why it pays off twice

The closing argument, which is also the bridge to the reflection modules:

> "It turns scattered tracking into one coherent picture that coaches you, and it makes every data point pay off twice: once as measurement, once as raw material for reflection."

## 4. The habit frameworks

### James Clear, Atomic Habits

Atomic Habits (2018) is the backbone of the habit system, and it is ingested rather than cited: the companion PDF is on file in Source Assets. Five ideas are load-bearing.

**Systems over goals.** The quote that appears throughout the deck:

> "You do not rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems."

**Identity-based habits.** Every habit is tied to an "I am someone who..." belief. This is not decoration. It is a column in the schema. Each of the seven sections has a habit table with the same columns:

> "What am I collecting / Process (the habit) / Insight / Outcome (the goal) / Identity (a 'belief about yourself' statement) / Daily, Weekly, Monthly, Annually / Time."

The worked example given for the Identity column is "I am someone who changes environments to generate better ideas," which is the Environmental Health habit table making the case for Safaris in the first person.

**The Four Laws of behavior change.** Make it obvious, make it attractive, make it easy, make it satisfying.

**Habit stacking.** Attach a new habit to an existing one.

**The two-minute rule**, and the compounding of 1 percent better. This is the same argument the timeboxing arc makes from the other direction: the hardest part of any task is starting, so make starting trivial.

### BJ Fogg, Tiny Habits

Also ingested, workbook on file. The Fogg Behavior Model: a behavior happens when Motivation, Ability and a Prompt come together at the same moment (B = MAP). The method is to anchor a tiny new behavior to something you already do, using the formula "After I [anchor], I will [tiny behavior]," keep it small enough that motivation is not needed, and celebrate right away to wire it in.

Where Clear and Fogg overlap, Clear supplies the identity layer and Fogg supplies the starting size. Where they differ, the system has taken Fogg's anchor formula and Clear's habit stacking as two names for close to the same move, and has not tried to reconcile the two vocabularies. That is a live inconsistency in the source material rather than a decision.

### How this became the cue cards

The cue cards are the teaching surface for the habits. The card contract, verbatim:

> "Inside each of the seven sections are 5 to 20 cue cards that teach one wellness practice each. Every card has four parts: 1) an Insight, 2) Why it's good for you, 3) Results you can expect, 4) the Routine."

The worked example in the deck is Meditation, laid out as a five-panel card: Insight, Why it's good for us, a brain image, Results you can expect, and Routine ("10 mins a day").

The format itself has a single named source, and the Reference Library states the attribution as a rule because it kept getting confused with the other learning app:

> "Imprint (visual micro-learning). The source and model for the Habit Building Cue Cards: the whole flashcard format comes from Imprint, not Elevate."

Elevate is a cognitive-training activity inside Mental Fitness. It is not the card format. Imprint also inspired the Year Compass exercises, which is why the reflection module and the habit modules look alike: they are the same borrowed pattern.

### What the modules actually do today

The methodology above is still the frame, and what shipped is narrower and more concrete than the deck's plan. There is a Cue Cards board (`habit-modules.html`, titled "1% Better Every Day") that acts as the hub: five cards, a daily x/4 meter and a streak badge. Four of the cards are tickable habit modules, each a daily card deck backed by Supabase and modeled on the AI Insights page: AI Insights, Atomic Habits (whose content is James Clear's 3-2-1 Thursday newsletter), Things Worth Knowing, and Compass. The fifth, Takeaways, is a browse-only library of 12 takeaway cards and 58 tiles cut from Scott's Gold Standard Master Cue Cards Library PDF, and it is the only part of the Habit Building Cue Cards deck as originally specified that actually exists. Ticking a card writes a completion with a duration, defaulting to ten minutes, which lands on the Time Bandit Wheel's outer ring and feeds the streak. The full module inventory, versions and live status are in 01-Product-Overview.md; the schema and the habit-name contract are in 03-Data-Model.md; the gap between the specified deck and the built one is tracked in 08-Roadmap-and-Open-Decisions.md.

## 5. The reflection frameworks

### The problem with YearCompass as given

YearCompass is a free annual reflection booklet, and the booklet PDF is on file in Source Assets. The design document is blunt about why it did not stick:

> "The YearCompass PDF questions are dry and long. A single one can need 20 minutes, which does not fit a 5-minute day, so the practice stalls."

The whole design follows from that sentence. The unit of reflection has to fit the day you actually have, not the day the booklet assumes.

### The seven design elements

1. **Visual, Imprint-style card.** One question at a time, clean and inviting, not a wall of text on a worksheet.
2. **Spiced-up wording.** Rewrite each question to be warmer and more evocative than the flat PDF phrasing, "so it pulls you in instead of feeling like homework."
3. **Sub-question ladder.** Reach each big question through two to four small warm-up sub-questions. "This kills the blank-page freeze."
4. **Time tags.** Every question carries an estimate: 5, 10 or 20 minutes. "This is what powers the skip."
5. **Skip to a Parking Lot, get a shorter one.** A "Only have 5 minutes?" skip moves today's question to a Parking Lot (not lost, it comes back later) and serves you another matched to the time you actually have.
6. **Answers compile into Project YOU.** Reflections accumulate as your own content and data over time, one source of truth.
7. **Same architecture as the cards.** A question database (question, spiced wording, sub-question ladder, category, time estimate, status), a daily surfacing, an Answered log and a Parking Lot.

### The ladder, worked

The ladder is the part most likely to be dropped by someone who thinks it is padding, so here is the example in full.

- **Original (dry):** "What were your biggest accomplishments this year?"
- **Spiced:** "Where did you surprise yourself this year?"
- **Ladder:**
  1. Name one moment you felt quietly proud, even a small one.
  2. What did it actually take from you to get there?
  3. Who noticed, or who would be proud if they knew?
  4. So, what were the wins that actually mattered to you this year?

> "The ladder does the warming up; by the time you reach the real question, you already have the raw material."

### How questions are chosen and resurfaced

Selection is by time tag against the time available. One question a day is surfaced; skipping parks it rather than discarding it, and the Parking Lot is the pool the shorter replacement is drawn from. Answered questions go to an Answered log so they are not re-asked.

Three questions were left open at design time and are worth naming as open rather than assumed: whether the 5/10/20 tagging is what drives the skip, whether parked questions should resurface on their own or sit until you go looking, and whether the bank stays YearCompass-only or grows into a general reflection question bank. Track these in 08-Roadmap-and-Open-Decisions.md.

There is also a recorded conflict on the unit of time. The Project YOU brief described the component as "turning the YearCompass booklet into daily 10-minute exercises," which is a single fixed size. The design document specifies 5, 10 or 20 minute tags, and the variable size is the entire point of the skip. The design document wins.

### Closing the QS loop

This is the most valuable idea in the reflection design, and it is what connects section 3 to section 5:

> "The real power: don't ask these questions cold. All year, Scott captures things that give him energy (the 'Gave me Energy' tracker and his wider Quantified Self logging). Pull that data in and use it to seed the answers."

In practice that means surfacing relevant captured moments when a question lands ("Here are the things that gave you energy this quarter, want to build your answer from these?"), and letting the ladder start from real entries rather than a blank page ("In March you logged X gave you energy. Why do you think that hit?"). The conclusion:

> "This turns reflection from memory-guessing into reviewing your own evidence, and it makes every QS entry pay off twice: once as data, once as raw material for reflection."

> "This is the whole system working as one: the tracking feeds the reflection, and the reflection feeds Project YOU."

The daily-questions design shipped as the Compass module. Note that the design document called Year Compass "the third instance of one shared pattern" after Clever Phrases and AI Daily. That count is now stale: the same daily-card engine runs at least four modules. The pattern claim is right, the number is not.

## 6. Social Well-Being and Connection

### Why people are tracked at all

Social Well-Being carries the strongest single piece of evidence in the system. The Harvard Study of Adult Development has run for 85 years, starting in 1938, following 724 people and later their descendants, and its central finding is that the strength of your relationships, more than wealth, fame or IQ, predicts health and happiness. Blue Zones adds the practice: the Okinawan "moai," a lifelong social circle, plus strong daily friendships and greeting others warmly.

If relationships are the largest lever, then leaving them to chance is the largest gap, and that is the argument for tracking them the same way workouts are tracked. This is also the section where tracking feels least natural and most transactional, which is why the design keeps the structure light and the entries small.

### The structure: Dunbar and the concentric circles

Dunbar's number supplies the shape: roughly 5 intimate, 15 close, 50 good friends, 150 stable relationships. The deck renders this as concentric circles of connection (inner 5, middle 15, affinity, outer) plus a personal Relationship Map. The circles are what turn a flat contact list into something you can act on, because they tell you which relationships are supposed to be maintained at what frequency, and they make an inner-circle relationship going quiet visible in a way an address book never does.

### The people to meet list

A deliberately minimal artifact: a numbered table with the columns `# | Name | Who they are / why | Status`. The design has three rules worth keeping.

- **The number is the ranking.** "#1 is who you most want to meet next." You renumber when priorities shift rather than adding a priority field.
- **Blank beats guessed.** The "who they are / why" and "Status" fields were left empty at creation rather than filled in with assumptions. That is a general rule in this system, not a one-off.
- **The list is not a task list.** When an entry becomes an action ("reach out to X", "get an intro to Y") it moves to the Social Well-Being section of the board. The list holds intent; the board holds work.

The file itself is a live personal list with a handful of names and no descriptive content, so there is nothing further to preserve from it here. Its stale reference to "the CrushingIt board" means the Today's Tasks board. In the current system this whole pattern has been superseded in implementation by the `connections` table and `connections.html`, fed by the capture pipeline; see 01-Product-Overview.md and 05-Capture-Pipeline.md.

### The Connection journal

A separate and much more sensitive artifact: a private relationship journal. **Its contents are personal and are not reproduced here or anywhere else in this library.** What is worth preserving is the design, because the design is unusually good and would be easy to get wrong on a rebuild.

The framing, verbatim:

> "This isn't a case being built for or against anything. It's a mirror. The goal is clarity and self-awareness, not a verdict."

The method: when something lands, dump it under whatever category fits, or drop it at the bottom under Unsorted and file it later. Date every entry. Over time the recurring themes surface on their own. The point is accumulation, not analysis in the moment.

The category schema, which is the reusable part:

| Section | Its job |
|---|---|
| Patterns I'm noticing | Left deliberately empty early on. Themes are read out of the entries later, not asserted up front. |
| What I need in a partner (my values) | The standard being measured against, written down separately from any specific incident. |
| Intimacy and affection | |
| Her world and stressors | Context on what the other person is carrying, "useful for seeing the whole picture, not just the friction." |
| Reasons I lean toward leaving | |
| Reasons I lean toward staying, what's working | |
| My own side (my triggers, my patterns, what I own) | |
| Questions to sit with | Open questions to revisit later, explicitly not to answer today. |
| Unsorted | The escape hatch that keeps capture frictionless. |

Two design principles hold the whole thing up, and both are about resisting the natural pull of the format. On why the "staying" column exists at all:

> "A one-sided ledger isn't self-awareness: it's a case file."

And on the hardest column:

> "My own side. The hardest and most valuable column. Where do I add to the dynamic? What are my own reactions telling me about me?"

A journal with only the grievance columns is a brief for the prosecution. The counterweight columns are what make it a mirror. If this is ever rebuilt as a page, those two columns are not optional.

**Handling rule.** This content is private. It currently exists as a plain markdown file in a folder, which conflicts with the standing privacy rule that sensitive personal notes live only in the RLS-locked database and never in memory or on any open page. Its contents must not be folded into any consolidated or published document. The concern also has three homes at once (the file, the board's private relationship capture area, and the board's Asa topics section), which is tracked as an open item in 08-Roadmap-and-Open-Decisions.md.

## 7. The Time Bandit Wheel

### The arithmetic

The wheel is a picture of an argument about time, and the argument is arithmetic before it is design.

The starting fact, from the timeboxing arc: a Harvard study found our minds wander about 47 percent of the time (Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010), and if we know what we should be doing at any moment, we are more likely to do it. Then the counting:

> "Every 24 hours holds 144 ten-minute segments; subtract sleep and you get roughly 30 ten-minute 'windows of opportunity' a day."

And from the Trackers List, describing the wheel itself:

> "A weekly wheel that divides your active day into 10-minute blocks and allocates them across categories. Built on the timeboxing math: about 96 ten-minute segments in a 16-hour active day (after 8 hours of sleep)."

Three numbers appear in the same units, so they need reconciling explicitly:

| Number | Where it comes from | What it counts |
|---|---|---|
| **144** | 24 hours divided by 10 minutes | Every ten-minute segment in a day, including sleep. The full denominator. |
| **96** | 16 hours divided by 10 minutes, after 8 hours of sleep | Every ten-minute segment of the active day. **This is the wheel's denominator.** |
| **roughly 30** | The deck's timeboxing slide | Not the same kind of number. It is the count of segments genuinely available to be spent deliberately, after the active day's committed time (work, meals, commuting, family logistics) is removed. It is the motivational figure, not the geometry. |

The deck never defines "window of opportunity," which is why the three numbers read as a contradiction. They are not. 144 is the day, 96 is the wheel, and roughly 30 is what is realistically yours. Anyone rebuilding the wheel should use 96 and should not try to make 30 appear anywhere in the drawing.

The point of counting in ten-minute units rather than hours is the starting problem, which is the same insight Atomic Habits reaches through the two-minute rule:

> "The hardest part of any task is starting, so start small: a 10-minute commitment is low enough that the brain doesn't resist, and starting is what leads to finishing."

Do that well and you become a "Time Bandit." The slogan is **"Dream big, execute small."** The message printed on the original paper wheel: "All it takes is 10 minutes to do something for you and feel better for it. Be a time bandit."

### The two rings

The original design, in one sentence:

> "The board is where you plan and check things off; the wheel is a picture of that same day drawn as a clock: the thick inner ring is your plan, the thin outer ring is what actually happened."

Under that model, the inner thick ring is the plan, drawn clockwise across the active day, with arc length equal to the time estimate (a 30-minute task is three blocks) and grey for free time. The outer thin ring, at one third the thickness, is the actual: positioned at the real clock time the thing finished, with length equal to how long it actually took. The reading rule is the whole payoff: aligned means on schedule, offset means it happened at a different time than planned. The wheel is a view, not a second list. It does not hold its own to-dos.

The live implementation has since changed what the inner ring means, deliberately, and this is the single most important thing to know before editing it:

- **Outer ring: actual time against the clock.** Habits and activities drawn at the hour they happened. Unchanged from the original design.
- **Inner donut: where the day went, proportions only.** No longer a clock-positioned plan. It is a pie.

Scott confirmed this split explicitly, and the standing instruction is: **do not try to reconcile them.** They encode different things on purpose. One is a timeline, the other is a budget. The implementation detail, the color mapping, the conflict rule that stops meals being double counted, and the live waking-day window are in 01-Product-Overview.md and 03-Data-Model.md.

Two smaller discrepancies are recorded rather than resolved. The active-day window is given as 6am to 10pm in the original explanation and 7am to 11pm in the live wheel; the live value governs. And the artifact has two names in the sources, "Habit Bandit Time Wheel" and "Time Bandit wheel." Both are in use. "HABIT BANDIT" is separately the name of the habit category on the board, which is probably where the crossover came from. The wheel is the Time Bandit Wheel.

## 10. Compass Sources, and how new source material enters the model

Section 2 defines the canonical seven. Section 8 lists the books and podcasts behind them. This section is the mechanism that joins the two. Compass Sources is where a new source is read, broken into points, and mapped onto the seven sections. Its own tagline states the relationship plainly: "A mechanism for importing new betterment insights and mapping them to the Clarity Compass sections. This is how the Compass sections were ultimately decided."

### What it is, structurally

Live at `blueprint.html`, in the Operating System nav under Maps, labelled **Compass Sources** since Aug 16 2026. It was called Knowledge Graph until then, which collided with a completely different page. That name now belongs to `knowledge-graph.html` alone.

It is a matrix, not a list. Sections are column groups, **tenets are the columns**, **sources are the rows**, and every filled cell is one source speaking to one tenet. Reading it as "sections with bullet points underneath" gets the model wrong.

As of Aug 18, 2026 it holds the seven life areas plus a holding pile called Not sure yet, 37 active tenets, 3 retired tenets that keep their codes forever, 9 sources and 59 notes of which 14 are placeholders carrying no insight. Coverage is uneven: Physical Health holds the most, Recreational Health the least, and eight tenets hold nothing at all. Every one of those figures is a query, not a typed number, and the page footer recomputes them on load.

**The page is generated, not typed.** Since v2.0 on Aug 16 2026 it holds no content of its own. It reads four tables at load: `kg_areas`, `kg_tenets`, `kg_sources` and `kg_links`. Adding a source is a handful of rows, not a republish, and the page shrank from 29,768 stored characters to 10,376 as a result. Cover artwork lives in `kg_sources.cover_url`, layered over a generated colour tile that shows through if a URL dies.

Not to be confused with `knowledge-graph.html`, now simply **Knowledge Graph**, which is a system architecture diagram of pages, tables and automations. The two are unrelated and the naming collision was resolved on Aug 16 2026.

**Every area and tenet carries a permanent code.** Areas are `A1` to `A7` plus `A0` for the holding pile. Tenets are `T001` upward, sources `S01` upward. The codes are deliberately meaningless: a code that named its area would go wrong the moment a tenet moved area, which the money and work rulings below caused twice in one day. A code is assigned once, never reused and never renumbered. The display label is free to change underneath it. A retired tenet keeps its code forever and records what it merged into, so a note filed years ago still resolves.

### The rules settled on Aug 16, 2026

**1. The canonical spelling is Social Well-Being, hyphenated.** This is a named exception to the standing writing rule against dashes: the hyphen is part of the name, not punctuation, and must never be "corrected" away. The Knowledge Graph is the surface that changes, because `todos`, `daily_template`, the Time Bandit Wheel palette and the `sync_social_wellbeing_completion` trigger all already use the hyphenated form, and that trigger matches the string exactly. Removing the hyphen anywhere upstream would stop social completions writing to `connections` and `daily_completed`, silently.

**2. Money belongs to Emotional Health.** Reasoning, in Scott's words: confidence in your financial plan is what brings the emotional wellbeing. Money is therefore not a candidate for an eighth section, now or later. Consequence: the "Material Goals" tenet moves from Recreational Health to join the money tenet in Emotional Health, and the surviving tenet is reframed away from "avoid stress" toward confidence in the plan.

**3. Work, meaning the current job and employer, belongs to Emotional Health.** As of Aug 16, 2026 that employer is Amazon. Reasoning, in Scott's words: having a job gives you emotional stability. Work is not a candidate for an eighth section either. Consequence: the Time Bandit Wheel Amazon slice can take the Emotional Health colour rather than the borrowed Amazon orange it uses today, because a life colour now exists for it.

**4. Adding a source never rewrites the graph.** Each source is a new row. Nothing already in the graph is edited by an addition. The work of adding a source is analysis and placement, not revision.

Rules 2 and 3 matter beyond their own subjects. Section 2 sets the test for an eighth section: it must have its own habits, its own trackers and its own evidence base. Money and work were the two strongest candidates in the system, both were examined on Aug 16, 2026, and both were absorbed into an existing section rather than promoted. The seven stand.

### The intake process, standing from Aug 16, 2026

For every new book, podcast, course or person:

1. Read the source and pull out its key points.
2. Decide, point by point, which existing tenets those points belong to, if any.
3. Present the findings as an interactive summary, one item per proposed tenet, with the reasoning visible.
4. Scott approves or rejects each item individually. Only approved items enter the canonical graph.
5. Anything that fits nowhere goes to the Outlier pile. A source is never allowed to invent a tenet on the day it arrives.

Seven supporting rules govern the quality of what enters:

1. Every source declares itself: title, type, author, year, and one line on what it is for. Four of the nine current sources have that last field blank.
2. Every cell carries an insight and a practice. No bare dots. 13 of the 62 current cells are content-free dots, 11 of them from one source.
3. A new source can never create a tenet on arrival. It maps to what exists, or it goes to Outlier.
4. Scott approves every point individually. Nothing enters without an explicit yes.
5. The Outlier pile is reviewed at 5 items or quarterly, whichever comes first. Three or more items circling the same idea is the evidence that justifies a new tenet, promoted deliberately.
6. Tenet wording never changes to accommodate a source. A source that repeatedly fits nowhere is information about the model, not a labelling problem.
7. Every count on the page is computed, never typed.

### The structural weakness to fix first

No tenet has a stable identifier. All 62 mappings point at a column position rather than a name that sticks, so inserting, removing or moving a tenet re-attaches mappings silently, with no error and no warning. Rules 2 and 3 turn this from theoretical into immediate, because both move content between sections.

The agreed shape of the fix: a permanent code for every section and every tenet, assigned once, never reused, never renumbered, with the display label free to change independently of it. The choice of code style is open and logged in 08. The recommendation on the table is a meaningless code such as `A1` to `A7` for sections and `T001` upward for tenets, precisely because a code that names its section becomes wrong the moment a tenet changes section, which rules 2 and 3 have just caused twice.
