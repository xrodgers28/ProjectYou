Project YOU · Knowledge Graph

How New Content Gets In v1.0

The seven gates every idea passes through before it becomes a row you can trust. Written in plain English, no database jargon required.

Aug 19, 2026 · EST Read from the live graph: 14 sources, 41 tenets, 84 rows, 28 approved

You hand me a name. "Read Deep Work." "That Huberman episode on morning light." "This blog post." What comes back is not a summary. It is a small set of finished, filed, checked ideas, each one already attached to the part of your Compass it belongs to, waiting for you to say yes or no. This page explains exactly what happens in between.

0First, what a "row" actually is

The graph has three moving parts. Getting these three straight makes everything else obvious.

The three parts

  • Source. The thing itself. A book, a podcast, a course, a person, a website. It gets a code like S12. You have 14.
  • Tenet. One of the 41 beliefs that make up your Clarity Compass, like "Sleep" or "Stay playful". Each one lives inside one of the 7 Compass areas.
  • Link. One idea, taken from one source, attached to one tenet. This is the row. You have 84, of which 28 are approved.

What one row contains

  • Headline. Four or five words. "Sleep on it, deliberately."
  • Insight. Two or three real sentences. Why it is true, where it came from, what it looks like in practice.
  • Practice. One thing you could do tomorrow.
  • Tenet. Which belief it strengthens.
  • Approved on. The date you said yes. Blank means it is not real yet.
One idea travels left to right. The Open Mode cue card already works exactly this way: edit the row, the card changes.

1The seven gates

Nothing gets written to the graph until it has survived all seven. Gates 1 to 6 are mine. Gate 7 is yours, and it is the only one that writes anything.

1
Mine · 2 minutes

Identify the source, and check you do not already have it

You say a name. I find the real thing: exact title, author, year, and what it is (book, podcast, course, person, website). Then I check it against the 14 sources already in the graph, including near-misses. Laurie Santos is in there twice on purpose, once as a course and once as a podcast, so overlap is allowed, but it has to be a decision rather than an accident. If it looks like something you already added under a different name, I say so before doing any work.

2
Mine · the slow bit

Actually read it, from real sources

I go and read. Not a memory dump of what I think the book says, but the actual material: the text, the transcript, the episode notes, reviews and interviews where the author explains themselves. This gate exists because of your standing rule that an anecdote has to be real. If I cannot confirm a story against a primary source, it does not go in the insight, however good it would have sounded.

3
Mine

Pull out 8 to 12 candidate ideas

Not the book's chapter headings. The ideas that survive the test "would this change something Scott does". A candidate has to be actionable, distinct from the others, and specific enough to argue with. Anything that reduces to "be more mindful" gets cut here, before it wastes your time. If a source only yields four good ones, you get four; padding a list to hit a number is how a graph fills up with noise.

4
Mine

Shape each one into the house format

Every candidate gets rewritten to the same shape so the graph reads as one voice rather than fourteen. Headline of four or five words. Insight of two or three sentences carrying the reasoning, the origin, or a real example, because a single line has never been enough for you to judge an idea. One practice line naming a single thing you could do tomorrow. No em-dashes.

5
Mine

File each one against a tenet

Each idea gets attached to one of your 41 tenets, which automatically places it in one of the 7 Compass areas. Where an idea genuinely fits two, I pick the one it strengthens most and note the second in the review, rather than filing it twice and inflating the count. Where nothing fits, it goes to the inbox tenet T000 Unfiled in the "Not sure yet" area, visibly, so it nags rather than hides.

The rule I will not break hereI never invent a new tenet. The 41 are your framework, not mine. If a source keeps pointing at a belief you do not have, I collect the evidence and propose it as a separate decision, with the rows that would sit under it, and you choose.
6
Mine

Check for duplicates, conflicts and gaps

Every candidate is compared against all 84 existing rows. Three outcomes get flagged on the review sheet before you see anything else:

  • Near-duplicate. Another source already says this. I show you both and you decide: keep both because two sources agreeing is itself useful, or cut one.
  • Conflict. This source contradicts something already in the graph. These are the most valuable rows you own and they never get quietly resolved. You get both, side by side.
  • Thin area. If the source lights up an area that is currently sparse, I say so, because that is the argument for reading it.
7
Yours · the only gate that writes

You approve, one card at a time

You get a published page, one card per candidate, and you tap Keep, Cut, or Edit. Nothing is in the graph until you do. Keeping a card stamps today's date into approved_on and the row goes live. Cutting one drops it entirely. Editing lets you fix the wording or change the tenet, and the version you tap is the version that gets saved, not my draft of it.

Three things I will never do without you Invent an anecdote or a statistic to make an insight land. Create a new tenet or a new Compass area. Write a row into the graph that you have not tapped Keep on. A source with no approved rows sits at zero, visibly, which is why S10, S11, S13 and S14 are showing zero right now rather than quietly filled in.

This is the design, not the build. Per the way we work, you see the mock and sign off before I write the real page. Each card carries the tenet it has been filed under, the flags from gate 6, and your three buttons.

Mockup · not live · Ingest review: S15 Deep Work, Cal Newport, 2016
A6 Mental Fitness · T030 Time affluence 2 of 9
Schedule the shallow work too

Newport's argument is that shallow work is not the enemy, unscheduled shallow work is, because email expands to fill whatever space you leave unnamed. He blocks it into the calendar with the same seriousness as the deep blocks, which is why his day has fewer hours of real focus than people assume but far more than most academics manage. The practical effect is that "I will get to it" stops being a category of time.

Try this: Give tomorrow's admin a named 30 minute block, then defend it like a meeting.
A7 Emotional Health · T035 Kindness toward yourself Near-duplicate of S12 · Being stuck is a stage
Boredom is training, not failure

Newport treats the reflex to reach for a phone in a queue as the thing that erodes concentration, because attention is trained by what you do in the gaps rather than what you do at the desk. Cleese arrives at a similar place from a completely different direction, calling discomfort a stage of the process rather than evidence it is going wrong. Two sources agreeing from opposite fields is usually a sign the idea is load-bearing.

Try this: Next queue you stand in, leave the phone in your pocket.

3The visuals that come with every source

Three, automatically, every time. The fourth, artwork per insight, only when a source is being turned into a cue card, because it is slow and most sources do not need it.

A. The Compass wheel map

Where a source actually lands. This is the real map of S12, John Cleese, showing the 18 approved rows spread across your seven areas. The instant read is that a book everyone files under "creativity" turned out to be four rows of Environmental Health and four of Sense of Purpose, which is not where anyone would have guessed.

Real data, read from kg_links on Aug 19, 2026. Pale ring shows the full radius; solid shows the hits.

B. The source to tenet flow

Which specific beliefs a source strengthens, and where it collides with a source you already have. The crossing lines are the interesting part: they are the places two authors are saying the same thing, which is your best signal that an idea is worth building a habit on.

Illustrative shape using a worked example. The real one is generated from the candidates.

C. The one-page source card

Cover image, author, year, the purpose line in one sentence, the approved rows, and the wheel map. This is the thing that goes into the Reference Library and gets linked on Mission Control, so a year from now you can remember why a source is in there without reopening the book.

4What happens after you approve

  • The rows go live with today's date stamped on them, and anything reading that source updates itself. The Open Mode cue card already works this way.
  • The source card is published and linked on Mission Control, per the standing rule that everything built gets a link there.
  • If the source earns a cue card, I say so and mock it up first rather than building it. The bar is roughly six or more approved rows that share a theme.
  • The graph counts get re-checked, so if an area is now thin by comparison, that becomes a reading suggestion rather than a surprise later.

5Something I found while writing this

I ran the approval check across the whole graph rather than trusting the row counts, and the result is worth your attention. Of your 84 rows, only 28 carry an approval date. The pattern is not random: S12, the Cleese book, is the one source that went through the full review, and it is 18 approved out of 18. Every source loaded before it has exactly one or two approved rows and the rest sitting unapproved, which is the signature of a bulk load rather than a review.

Nothing is broken. The rows are there and the cue cards read them. But it means the graph currently mixes two grades of content, and only one grade has actually been through you. That is the argument for the skill, and it also gives you a second job to consider: a catch-up pass over the older sources using the same approval sheet.
